What Actually Happens in a Security Audit (We Walked Through One in Class)

August 24, 2026

"Security audit" is one of those phrases that sounds like it belongs to a specialist team in a locked room somewhere — intimidating, technical, out of reach for a beginner. So for one class, Cybersecurity & DevOps instructor Brian Kiptoo decided to demystify it the only way that actually works: by running one, live, in front of students, on a deliberately vulnerable practice application.

Step one: it starts with mapping, not hacking

The first surprise for most students is that a real audit doesn't start with dramatic exploits. It starts with mapping — going through the application methodically to understand what it does, what data it handles, and where it talks to the outside world. "Most beginners picture a security audit as someone typing fast and breaking in," Brian says. "In reality, most of the actual finding happens before anyone tries to break anything. You're looking for what's exposed, not guessing."

Step two: testing the obvious things first

Once the map was in place, the class worked through the basics that catch a surprising number of real production apps: unvalidated user input, exposed error messages that reveal too much about the system, and permissions that are looser than they should be. None of it required advanced tools — just a methodical checklist and the patience to test each item properly.

Step three: writing it up

The part students found most unexpected was the last one: a real audit ends in a written report, not a triumphant screenshot. Every finding gets documented with how severe it is, how it could be exploited, and how to fix it — because the point of an audit isn't to prove you can break something, it's to make the system safer once you're done.

By the end of the session, several students admitted the whole process felt far more approachable than they expected. "It's not magic," one student said afterward. "It's just being thorough and asking the right questions in order." Which, as Brian points out, is most of what the job actually is.
